How to use the Kreaton console

Kreaton watches UPI payments as they are authorised and stops the ones that are scams. This guide takes you from never having seen it to reading every screen, running your own data through it, and knowing what each number means. It assumes you know nothing about fraud systems.

Live at kreaton-upi.vercel.app · no login · works on a phone
Second edition, September 2026. Adds the Your data screen, the single-payment composer, the durable audit record, and what changed on the console because of them.

What this is

In most fraud, someone steals your card or your password and spends your money. Banks are good at catching that, because you did not make the payment.

An authorised push payment scam is different, and it is the one this system exists for. Someone calls pretending to be the police, a bank officer, an employer or a relative, and talks the victim into paying them. The victim taps the buttons themselves — their PIN, their phone, their bank app. To the bank it looks like an ordinary transfer, so it goes through, and by the time anyone reports it the money has been moved through a chain of accounts and withdrawn.

Kreaton reads every payment at the moment it is authorised — in well under a millisecond — and decides whether it should go through, whether the payer should be asked to confirm, or whether it should be stopped. It does that by weighing twelve signals: how unusual the amount is for this payer, whether they are on a call right now, how old the receiving account is, how fast that account forwards money onward, and so on.

Everything shipped with the console is simulated. The payments, the people and the scams are generated from a synthetic dataset, so no real person's money or data appears anywhere. The figures are real measurements of the system, taken on that synthetic data. If you bring your own file (page 9), the console then measures on that instead — and tells you so on every screen.

The three outcomes

Every payment ends up in exactly one of three states. These three words, and the three shapes beside them, appear on every screen — learn them once and the rest of the console reads itself.

OutcomeWhat it meansHow often
Let throughMatches how this payer normally pays. It settles, untouched.Almost every payment.
Held to verifySomething is off. The payer is asked to confirm who they are paying — in a way the fraudster on the phone cannot simply relay — before it moves.A few in a thousand.
BlockedAlmost certainly a scam. The money never leaves the account.A few in a thousand.

Your first five minutes

1. Open the console

Go to kreaton-upi.vercel.app. You land on the console with every counter at zero. Nothing is broken; nothing has been replayed yet.

2. Press Watch it run

Payments start flowing through the engine at six per second. Each one is scored, decided, and written to the audit record as it passes. The clock in the top right is the engine's clock — it moves in payment time, not real time, so it runs faster than yours.

3. Let it run for about thirty seconds

You will see the Let through counter race ahead while Held and Blocked barely move. That imbalance is the whole problem in one picture: scams are rare, and a system that intervened too eagerly would block thousands of real customers to catch a handful of scams.

4. Read the six statistics

FigureWhat it tells you
Payments screenedHow many have gone through the engine, and their total value.
Scams caughtOf the scams in the stream so far, how many were held or blocked. Shown only when the data has ground truth.
Genuine payments stoppedThe false-positive rate. Every one is a real customer inconvenienced, so lower is better.
Money protectedMoney that would have been lost had the payment gone through, counted conservatively after assuming some would have been recovered anyway.
Decision timeHow long one decision takes, at the 99th percentile — the slow ones, not the average.
Audit entriesRecords in the tamper-evident chain, and the fingerprint of the latest one.

5. Use the feed filter

Below the chart, the feed lists every payment. Since almost all of them are let through, press Only the ones it stopped to see just the interventions. This is the fastest way to see what the system is for.

6. Click any row

The panel on the right fills in with why that payment got that decision. On a wide screen it stays beside the feed as you scroll; on a narrow one it comes to you.

The chart

The two curved lines are the decision boundaries. Above the lower line a payment is held; above the upper line, blocked. They slope down to the right because the boundary moves with the amount: the cost of wrongly approving a ₹5 lakh payment is far higher than wrongly approving ₹500, while the cost of wrongly holding either is about the same. The lines are not a threshold somebody picked — they fall out of the cost arithmetic, and they move when you change the policy in the dropdown.

Try a scam on it yourself

Scams are rare in the stream, so a live demonstration would wait a long time to see one arrive on its own. The injector builds one on demand against a real payer from the feed, with the context fingerprint that scam actually leaves, and pushes it through the same path as everything else.

ScenarioWhat it isWhat should happen
Digital arrestA victim held on a video call by someone posing as police, told to transfer savings to a "verification" account.Blocked. Call, urgency, novelty and amount all fire.
KYC updateAn urgent message that the account will be frozen unless a fee is paid to a pasted identifier.Blocked.
StructuringAn extraction split into payments each below a monitored limit.The first passes; the window then crosses the threshold and the next is blocked.
Split re-attemptA held payment retried in two halves to a fresh account.Held, then linked to the open hold, then escalated.

Legitimate large paymentA big payment to a beneficiary the payer has paid for months.Let through.

Try "Context suppression" if you only try one. It is the case the system is worst at, and it is in the menu on purpose. A fraud tool that only demonstrates its successes is not telling you what it is worth. Run it on Balanced, then change the policy dropdown to Liability-first and inject it again.

Your data new in this edition

The console ships with a slice of the generated corpus. The Your data screen lets you replace it with a file of your own, or compose a single payment by hand and watch the decision move as you change it. Both run through exactly the same engine as everything else — nothing on this screen is a simulation of the product.

Replaying a file

Two samples are built in because they sit at opposite ends of what an import can tell you. The bank statement is what most people actually have: a date, a narration, debit and credit columns, a running balance — and no payer column, no beneficiary number, no session context. The UPI log is what a payment provider sees at authorisation, including the call and entry-method fields no public dataset carries.

Step 1 — confirm the mapping

The importer reads your column headings and proposes which engine field each one feeds. You confirm or change every line before anything is scored. This matters: bind the wrong column to the payer and every behavioural baseline is rebuilt against the wrong identity, and no number downstream would look wrong.

Two things a statement lacks are handled rather than refused. With no payer column, the whole file is read as one account holder — which is what a statement is. With no beneficiary number, the narration folded to a stable form becomes the identity, so "RAMESH TRADERS", "Ramesh Traders" and "UPI/RAMESH TRADERS." are one beneficiary rather than three. That is what lets the receiving-side signals work on a statement at all. Only the amount has no substitute.

Step 3 — read what the file cannot show

This is the most important part of the screen, and it cannot be skipped.

Almost no real file carries UPI session context. A bank statement does not know whether the payer was on a call, whether the identifier was pasted, whether the device was new. When those columns are absent the engine does not treat them as neutral — it sees every payment as though the attacker had suppressed every indicator they control. That is the hardest case for the model and the weakest one in the adversarial report. A detection rate measured on such a file is a floor, not a representative figure, and the console says so beside the number rather than letting it stand alone.

Step 4 — replay it

To go back to the shipped corpus, press Back to the shipped corpus on either screen. Your imported file is kept in the browser, so a refresh no longer costs you the file you just mapped — see "Things that will confuse you".

Composing one payment

The importer answers "how does the engine behave on my data". The second tab answers the other question people ask at a demonstration: "what happens if I change this one thing?"

Composed payments run through the real authorisation path, so they land in the feed and in the audit ledger like any other payment. Choose a payer from the feed to score against a real history; "someone with no history" is a legitimate thing to test, but amount deviation and time-of-day surprisal then have almost nothing to measure against.

Glossary

Scam risk p(fraud)
The engine's estimate that this payment is a scam, from under 0.001% to near 100%. Most payments sit far below 1%.
Genuine payments stopped false positive rate
The share of legitimate payments that got interrupted. Every one is a real customer inconvenienced, so lower is better.
Precision
Of the payments the system stopped, the share that really were scams. High precision means staff are not wading through false alarms.
Money protected liability avoided
Money that would have been lost and not recovered, had the payment gone through. Counted conservatively.
Mule account
An account that exists to receive scam money and pass it on quickly, often opened with a real person's stolen or rented identity.
Hops
How many accounts the money has been forwarded through. More hops means less chance of freezing it in time.
Step-up
The extra confirmation asked of a payer when a payment is held — chosen so the fraudster on the phone cannot simply relay it.
Contribution nats, log-odds
How much one signal moved the verdict. Positive pushes toward scam, negative toward legitimate. They add up exactly to the total.
Calibration
Whether the stated probabilities are honest. A well-calibrated 10% means it happens about one time in ten.
Decision time latency p99
How long one decision takes. "p99" means 99 out of 100 were at least this fast — fairer than the average, which hides the slow ones.
Ground truth
Whether the data says which payments really were scams. The shipped corpus has it throughout; your file may not, in which case accuracy is not reported at all.
Quiet value
What the engine assumes for a session field your file did not supply: no call, typed identifier, trusted device. Each is the reading an attacker would want.

Things that will confuse you

Reloading the page resets the shipped replay — but keeps your file

The engine runs inside your browser tab. A full reload (F5) sets the shipped replay back to zero. If you had imported a file, that file and how far you had replayed it are kept in the browser and restored automatically. Moving between pages using the links at the top keeps everything either way.

The clock is not real time

Playback is measured in payments per second, not minutes. The shipped window covers about four hours of traffic, so the clock in the top right jumps forward much faster than a real one. With an imported file the clock follows the file's own timestamps.

The dates are in 2026

The synthetic dataset was generated with a 2026 window. Nothing is wrong with your computer.

Scams you inject do not change the score

Injected and composed payments are marked as such and excluded from the measured statistics. If they counted, anyone could inflate the detection rate by injecting twenty obvious scams.

"Scams caught" says "—" on your file

Your file has no ground-truth column, so the console does not know which payments were scams and will not guess. You still get every decision and every reason; you do not get a score for them.

Nothing happens for a long time

That is the correct behaviour, not a broken page. Scams are a small fraction of payments. Use the feed filter or inject a scenario if you want to see interventions immediately.

Where it lives

The site is hosted on Vercel at kreaton-upi.vercel.app, built from the apps/web folder of the repository. The code is on GitHub at junekashif/Kreaton; the submission copy is Cognitia-IEM/KREATON. Pushing to the main branch runs the checks and, if they pass, redeploys.

The API

GET  /api/v1/health            model and policy digests, ledger head, persistence status
GET  /api/v1/authorize         a complete example request body
POST /api/v1/authorize         authorise one payment
POST /api/v1/authorize/batch   authorise an ordered run, state carrying forward
POST /api/v1/import            match a file's columns and report; optionally replay it

Where decisions are kept

The engine holds its working state in memory, because an authorisation has a budget of a few milliseconds. Durability sits behind that: after each decision has been returned, the sealed audit record, the payment and any hold are copied to a Google Sheet, in batches, so a judge or an auditor can open the record in a browser without being given a query tool. If the copy fails the decision is unaffected; the health endpoint shows failed and dropped counts and the last error. Setup — one service account, one shared spreadsheet, two environment variables — is in docs/PERSISTENCE.md.

They are stored as secrets rather than written into the code because everything in the repository is readable by anyone. Without them the deploy job skips itself and the site stays up as it was; you deploy by hand with vercel deploy --prod from the repository root.
